// PROBLEM

8–10 hours wasted per week, per change management employee.

The question: Can we just… automate the procedures update process with AI?

For this project, our org's leadership asked us if we could use these new shiny AI tools to automate a painfully tedious process at the bank.

That process was: at Wells Fargo, every job function had to be documented in formal procedures, reviewed and approved before any technology change could ship.

The tricky part was staying on top of what was actually changing. Change management employees had no good way of knowing when a tech update might affect a procedure they owned.

So, they did the only thing they could: show up to every single release demo call and pay close attention. Sometimes those calls ran for hours. We estimated it was eating 8 to 10 hours a week per person, just to stay informed…. oof 😅

Team: 5 developers, a PO, a Scrum master, and me, the UXer!

[01]

Discovery

7 stakeholder interviews: change management, the policy/regulatory team, and teams building internal tools.

Before a single wireframe could be drawn, we had to understand the process we were designing for.

So, as the solo UXer on the initiative, I led the stakeholder interviews that allowed us to map the process and scope our MVP.

  • Seek buy-in
  • Map the process
  • Get technical input

Meanwhile, on those discovery calls, my job was asking the right probing questions to understand how this work was being done. Afterwards, I'd synthesize what we heard into something we could actually design against.

Some of our key findings were:

  • → The approval chain was more layered and regulated than anyone had briefed us on
  • → The legacy procedures tool was on its way out — a new internal platform was in development
  • → The existing workflow touched Jira, SharePoint forms, email, and the legacy tool
  • → Nobody in Small Business had ever mapped this end to end before…
[02]

Define

Given what we know now, how do we apply this to our product thinking?

Key takeaway: focus on what we can automate today vs. tomorrow.

Discovery made one thing clear: we couldn't automate the full process, and it wouldn't be strategic to try.

The approval chain was pretty heavily regulated, and the legacy tooling situation made deep integration impractical for MVP.

[ Image: MVP scoping graphic — skateboard → scooter → bike → car ]

So we scoped to what we could actually impact: the monitoring and alerting layer. Catch the changes early, route them to the right person, give them something actionable. Everything downstream stays exactly as it was.

The process couldn't change, so we focused on what we could actually impact. Two levels of approval were non-negotiable, and it was unlikely we'd be able to automate this on day one. For MVP, we realized we should focus on monitoring & alerting.

Design

Once the process was mapped, the wireframing took two days. That's not a flex — it's a reflection of how much clarity the discovery work had bought us.

Inspired by a Grammarly-like experience.

[ Image: Grammarly-inspired interaction reference ]

The Feed
A table-style landing page showing all procedures flagged for review: procedure name, owner, category, last updated date, status, and an AI confidence score giving users an at-a-glance read on which updates needed careful attention and which were routine.

The Review View
Clicking in opened a summary of changes first, then an optional full text view: side-by-side comparison of the existing and AI-recommended procedure, with removals in red strikethrough and additions in green underline.

[04]

Handoff + Iterate

A few rounds of feedback from the engineering team shaped some of the details. Two design decisions that came up in this process:

#1) What if one code change impacts multiple procedures? What if one procedure is impacted by multiple code changes?

When a single change impacted multiple procedures, the summary view surfaced each one as a distinct row on the user's dashboard. This is because from the user's perspective, they care about what they need to do, not what's happening underneath.

[ Image: Dashboard highlighting multiple flagged procedures from a single change ]

#2) What if the same procedure is impacted by multiple code changes?

From the user's perspective, they'll want to see any changes to a single procedure in one place — but they'll also need transparency as to what prompted the change and why. We added a tab design to show transparency: basically the “source code” that prompted the change. This could get complicated fast, but for MVP it was good enough!

[ Image: Edge case — tabbed view showing source-code context for a procedure change ]

Outcome + Next Steps

This was the first AI-assisted internal tool we built in Small Business, so on those grounds alone it felt like a huge feat!

Early user feedback was positive, but I ended up being added to another project before being able to totally see it through.
